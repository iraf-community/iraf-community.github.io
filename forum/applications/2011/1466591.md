**emma** wrote on Jun 07, 2011

{% raw %}
<div>
Hi Charly,<br>
<br>
Sorry for the delay in my reply. I have been speaking with the GMOS Instrument Scientist (IS) about your questions and I have some answers for you.<br>
<br>
Firstly, your flat has been over-fitted. This means that the fringes present in your flat were fitted in addition to the continuum. Only the continuum of the flat needs to be fit. This over-fitting caused the weird features that you noticed in your normalised flat. Due to the fringing present in your flat frames, the IS recommends fitting the flat detector by detector. To do this you need to set the fl_detec parameter to yes (apologies to telling you to set this to no in an earlier post). When fitting the first extension (CCD1) of your flat (i.e., the red end, which is where the fringing is at its worse) you should use a low order so that you don't fit the fringes. An order of 5-7 should suffice. The second extension (CCD2) of the flat has less fringing, but in order not to fit the fringes, use an order of 10-15. The third extension (CCD3) of the flat (i.e., the blue end) shouldn't have much fringing at all. This is when it is ok to use an order of ~29. This will fit the structure (that doesn't include fringing) in the flats as mentioned in a previous post.<br>
<br>
The result of running gsflat with the fl_detec parameter set to yes means that the output "combflat" will not be gmosaiced, which will cause problems when running gscut (as you have already discovered). To resolve this issue, just run gmosaic on the "combflat" before running gscut. <br>
<br>
I also spoke to the IS about your combining question. There are two methods you can try; you can use gemcombine to combine your transformed spectra (i.e., the output from gstransform) prior to extraction (set the gemcombine.offsets parameter to "wcs") OR you can extract the individual spectra and then use scombine to combine the extracted spectra. You can test both methods and decide for yourself which you prefer :)<br>
<br>
I hope that answers all your questions.<br>
<br>
Many thanks,<br>
<br>
Emma :)

Your flat definitely looks better. Just to re-iterate, only the continuum of the flat needs to be fit ... you should still have fringes present in your processed, normalised flat. However, the orders I specified in my last post work the best for the R400 grating. I realise now that your data uses the B600 grating. The fringes are not as severe with the B600 grating as they are with the R400 grating, so it is ok to use a higher order when fitting the continuum. For the B600 grating, using an order of 15-19 for all detectors should work well.<br>
<br>
Also, I was just reminded about the talks that are available at <a href="http://www.noao.edu/meetings/gdw/">http://www.noao.edu/meetings/gdw/</a> ... in particular, Kathy Roth's talk (<a href="http://www.noao.edu/meetings/gdw/files/Roth_NS_Reduction.pdf">http://www.noao.edu/meetings/gdw/files/Roth_NS_Reduction.pdf</a>) shows step-by-step instructions on how to fit a flat for the R400 grating. Remember that since the B600 grating does not show as severe fringing as the R400 grating that you should use higher orders when fitting B600 spectroscopic flats.<br>
